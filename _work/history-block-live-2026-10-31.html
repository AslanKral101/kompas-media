Chapter 01 Origins 1917

Born Over Wartime London

In 1917, as German raids reached London, the Royal Flying Corps introduced "colour change clocks" to keep track of enemy aircraft.

Its idea was simple: instead of writing down the exact minute of every sighting, give each report a colour. Time became something you could see.

  1. 1917German bombers reach London. The Royal Flying Corps introduces colour change clocks to track them.
  2. Aug 1917Major-General Edward Ashmore takes command of the new London Air Defence Area.
  3. 1 Apr 1918The Royal Flying Corps becomes the Royal Air Force, and the clock goes with it.

Chapter 02 The Instrument 1917–1945

How The Sector Clock Worked

Divided into bold colour coded five minute segments, the Sector Clock turned time into an instant visual reference, allowing controllers to judge recency at a glance.

  • Five Minute TrianglesThe dial is ringed with red, yellow and blue triangles, each one covering five minutes.
  • Three Colours, RepeatingThe sequence repeats every fifteen minutes, so every report falls into a colour.
  • 12 And 24 Hour RingsAn outer 12 hour ring for the minute hand and an inner 24 hour ring for military time.
  • Built To Be ReadLarge numerals and strong colours, readable across an operations room and in low light.
  1. 1 · A Report ArrivesA sighting is logged with the colour of the triangle under the minute hand.
  2. 2 · A Coloured CounterAt sector headquarters, a counter in that same colour is placed on the map table.
  3. 3 · Age At A GlanceComparing counter colours with the clock shows instantly which reports are fresh and which are old.

Chapter 03 Legacy 1917–1991

One Idea, Many Faces

The RAF and the Royal Observer Corps kept using sector clocks well into the Cold War. Versions differed: RAF clocks carried the King's Crown beneath the 24, Observer Corps clocks did not, and American forces adapted the design with coloured blocks instead of triangles. Today, original clocks are prized by collectors.

    Chapter 04 Operations Rooms 1930s

    Built for Real Environments

    In the early years of Britain's air defence network, the Sector Clock became a key tool for synchronising operations. By dividing time into bold visual segments, it allowed teams to instantly distinguish between current and outdated reports, reducing hesitation and enabling faster, more coordinated responses.

    Chapter 05 The System 1936

    The Dowding System

    The Sector Clock operated within what became known as the Dowding System, a pioneering network built by Air Chief Marshal Hugh Dowding after RAF Fighter Command was formed in 1936. Radar detection, human verification and coloured time coding reduced the gap between sighting and interception to mere minutes.

    1. DetectChain Home radar along the coast, the Observer Corps over land.
    2. FilterThe Filter Room at Bentley Priory turns raw reports into single tracks.
    3. PlotGroup and Sector Operations Rooms plot raids and scramble fighters.

    Chapter 06 The People 1939

    The Women of the Sector Clock

    In RAF Operations Rooms, women of the Women's Auxiliary Air Force transformed live reports into colour coded movements, bringing the Sector Clock to life. Its effectiveness relied on their speed, accuracy, and discipline in real time coordination.

    Controllers and duty officers looked down on the whole table from the raised dais and directed the response.

    Each plotter was linked by telephone to her own reporting line, hearing new positions the moment they came in.

    Long rakes let plotters push markers to the centre of the table without leaning over it, keeping the picture current minute by minute.

    The group's area drawn on a grid. One look showed every raid, where it was and how fresh the information was.

    Each marker carried the raid's details. Its colour came from the sector clock at the moment the report arrived.

    Chapter 07 The Battle 1940

    The Battle of Britain

    In 1940, the Dowding System, Sector Clock, and WAAF worked as one, combining radar, human coordination, and speed. This allowed RAF fighters to respond within minutes, playing a decisive role in the Battle of Britain.
